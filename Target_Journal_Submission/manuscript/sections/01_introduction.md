## 1. Introduction

District heating supplies space heating and hot water through a network of pipes and customer substations, and it is a central part of plans for low-carbon heat [Lund2014_4GDH, Lund2021_4GDH5GDHC, Werner2017_review]. Each substation is a small control system: a controller sets target temperatures, and valves, pumps and heat exchangers try to reach them. When a part fails or a setting is wrong, the customer may get a cold home or no hot water, and the network may receive return water that is too warm. Warm return water raises the cost of supplying heat and makes it harder to use low-temperature and excess heat [Gadd2014_lowreturn, Averfalk2020_economic]. Faults are common: threshold checks on meter data found faults in 74% of 135 Swedish substations [Gadd2015_faultdetection]. In practice many faults are found only when a customer calls.

Utilities now record 10-minute data from their substations, so they could detect faults earlier. Many data-driven detectors have been proposed, most of them unsupervised normal-behaviour models such as autoencoders, because labelled faults are scarce [Neumayer2023_survey]. A detector, however, is only useful if the operator can follow up its alarms. Industrial experience shows that alarm systems often produce more alarms than people can handle [Wang2016_alarms, Beebe2013_alarmfloods]. The questions an operator asks are therefore practical. How many faults will this detector find before the customer calls? How many false alarms will it raise per substation and year? Will it still work on substations from another controller manufacturer?

Published evaluations rarely answer these questions. The PreDist dataset, which its authors describe as the first public, labelled dataset of this kind, evaluates detectors on 7-day windows before fault reports and on a similar number of hand-picked normal weeks [PreDist2026]. Its authors note that this balanced set overestimates precision, because faults are much rarer in real operation [PreDist2026]. Other studies report case studies, substation-level accuracy or rankings (Section 2). None of these gives the false-alarm burden in continuous operation, and none tests whether thresholds chosen on one fleet carry over to another.

This paper evaluates fault detectors the way an operator would run them. Every 10-minute step of every substation is scored, alarms are counted per substation-year, and each detector is compared at fixed false-alarm budgets of 1, 2 and 4 alarms per substation-year. All thresholds are chosen on the substations of one manufacturer (35 substations) and applied once, unchanged, to the 58 substations of the other manufacturer. This test was pre-registered and locked before any detector was run. We compare eleven detectors that differ in what they monitor, how they represent the signals and which model they use, including the autoencoder published with the dataset.

The main contributions are:

1. **A continuous, budgeted and locked evaluation protocol for substation fault detection.** It reports recall at a fixed number of false alarms per substation-year, freezes thresholds on one manufacturer before testing on another, and compares every detector with random alarms raised at the same rate. Unlike the balanced event protocol, it measures the alarm burden an operator would face.
2. **A pre-registered cross-manufacturer benchmark of eleven detectors.** On the held-out manufacturer the best detector finds 7 of 25 faults within four false alarms per substation-year. Only 3 of 33 detector settings beat random alarms at the same rate, and the ranking of detectors on the development fleet does not predict their ranking on the new one.
3. **An analysis of why detectors fail to transfer.** Most detections on the new fleet happen in the first 90 days of a substation's data, where commissioning changes dominate; autoencoder false alarms follow seasonal changes of operation; and restricting monitoring to the comfort loops helps on threshold-free ranks but not at operational budgets.
4. **A rule for how large a benchmark must be to certify an alarm budget.** With n normal weeks and no false alarm among them, the 95% upper bound on the operator's alarm burden is 52.2·(1 − 0.05^{1/n}) alarm-weeks per substation-year. The 30 normal weeks of the PreDist test manufacturer can certify no better than 5.0; certifying one alarm-week per year needs at least 155 clean normal weeks.
